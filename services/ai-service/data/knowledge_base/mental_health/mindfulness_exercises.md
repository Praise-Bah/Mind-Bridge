# Guided Mindfulness Exercises for Students

## Introduction

These five exercises are designed to be practical and accessible. You can do them in your dorm room, in a quiet corner on campus, or even at your desk. No special equipment is needed. Each exercise includes step-by-step instructions so you can practice on your own.

---

## Exercise 1: The 5-4-3-2-1 Grounding Exercise

**Best for:** Acute anxiety, panic, feeling overwhelmed, dissociation
**Duration:** 3-5 minutes

This sensory grounding exercise anchors you to the present moment by engaging all five senses. It is especially effective when anxiety is high or when you feel disconnected from reality.

### Instructions

1. Pause whatever you are doing. If possible, sit down. Take one slow, deep breath.

2. **5 things you can SEE:** Look around and name five things you can see. Be specific. For example: "the blue pen on my desk," "the pattern on the curtain," "sunlight on the wall," "my water bottle," "the green of the trees outside."

3. **4 things you can TOUCH:** Notice four things you can physically feel right now. For example: "my feet on the floor," "the fabric of my shirt," "the smooth surface of my phone," "the warmth of my hands."

4. **3 things you can HEAR:** Listen carefully and name three sounds. For example: "birds outside," "the hum of a fan," "voices in the distance."

5. **2 things you can SMELL:** Notice two smells. If you cannot detect any, move to something nearby and smell it — your clothes, a book, food, or soap on your hands.

6. **1 thing you can TASTE:** Notice one thing you can taste. It might be the lingering taste of a meal, toothpaste, or simply the taste of your mouth. You can take a sip of water and focus on the taste.

7. Finish by taking three slow, deep breaths. Notice how you feel compared to when you started.

## Exercise 2: Box Breathing (Square Breathing)

**Best for:** Calming the nervous system before exams, presentations, or stressful situations
**Duration:** 3-5 minutes

Box breathing is used by military personnel, athletes, and first responders to stay calm under pressure. It works by regulating the autonomic nervous system.

### Instructions

1. Sit upright in a comfortable position. Place your hands on your knees or in your lap. Close your eyes or soften your gaze.

2. **Inhale** slowly through your nose for a count of 4. Imagine drawing the first side of a square as you breathe in.

3. **Hold** your breath for a count of 4. Imagine drawing the second side of the square.

4. **Exhale** slowly through your mouth for a count of 4. Imagine drawing the third side of the square.

5. **Hold** your breath (lungs empty) for a count of 4. Imagine drawing the fourth side, completing the square.

6. Repeat this cycle 4-6 times, or until you feel noticeably calmer.

**Tips:**
- If 4 seconds feels too long, start with 3 seconds per side and work up.
- You can visualize the square in your mind or trace one on your desk with your finger.
- Try this for 2 minutes before walking into an exam room.

## Exercise 3: Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)

**Best for:** Physical tension, insomnia, chronic stress, headaches
**Duration:** 10-15 minutes

PMR involves systematically tensing and then releasing each muscle group. The contrast between tension and relaxation teaches your body what true relaxation feels like and releases stored physical stress.

### Instructions

1. Find a quiet, comfortable place. Sit or lie down. Close your eyes and take three deep breaths.

2. **Feet:** Curl your toes tightly. Hold the tension for 5 seconds. Notice the feeling of tightness. Then release completely. Notice the contrast — the feeling of relaxation. Breathe.

3. **Lower legs:** Tense your calf muscles by pointing your toes toward your head. Hold for 5 seconds. Release. Breathe.

4. **Upper legs:** Squeeze your thigh muscles tightly. Hold for 5 seconds. Release. Breathe.

5. **Stomach:** Tighten your abdominal muscles as if bracing for impact. Hold for 5 seconds. Release. Breathe.

6. **Chest:** Take a deep breath and hold it, tensing your chest. Hold for 5 seconds. Exhale and release. Breathe normally.

7. **Hands:** Clench your fists tightly. Hold for 5 seconds. Release and let your fingers spread naturally. Breathe.

8. **Arms:** Tense your biceps by bending your arms and making a "strongman" pose. Hold for 5 seconds. Release. Breathe.

9. **Shoulders:** Raise your shoulders up toward your ears as high as you can. Hold for 5 seconds. Drop them. Breathe.

10. **Face:** Scrunch up your entire face — eyes, nose, mouth. Hold for 5 seconds. Release and let your face go completely soft. Breathe.

11. Take a few moments to scan your body. Notice the overall sense of relaxation. Take three final deep breaths before gently opening your eyes.

**Tip for students:** PMR is excellent to do in bed if you are having trouble falling asleep.

## Exercise 4: Guided Body Scan Meditation

**Best for:** General relaxation, body awareness, stress reduction, pre-sleep calm
**Duration:** 10-20 minutes

Unlike PMR, the body scan does not involve tensing muscles. Instead, you simply bring gentle awareness to each part of your body.

### Instructions

1. Lie down on your back, arms by your sides, palms facing up. Close your eyes. Take three deep breaths and allow your body to settle.

2. **Top of head:** Bring your attention to the crown of your head. Notice any sensations — warmth, tingling, pressure, or nothing at all. Simply observe.

3. **Forehead and eyes:** Notice if you are holding tension in your forehead or around your eyes. Breathe gently and invite softening.

4. **Jaw and mouth:** Many people hold tension in their jaw without realizing it. Let your teeth part slightly. Let your tongue rest softly in your mouth.

5. **Neck and throat:** Notice any tightness. Imagine your breath flowing into this area, gently releasing tension.

6. **Shoulders and upper back:** These areas carry a lot of stress. Observe without trying to change anything.

7. **Arms and hands:** Feel the weight of your arms resting. Notice the temperature of your hands, any tingling in your fingers.

8. **Chest and upper back:** Notice the gentle rise and fall of your breathing.

9. **Stomach and lower back:** Observe any sensations — hunger, fullness, tightness, warmth.

10. **Hips and pelvis:** Notice the weight of your body against the surface beneath you.

11. **Legs:** Slowly scan through your thighs, knees, calves, and shins.

12. **Feet and toes:** Bring your attention all the way down to the soles of your feet and each toe.

13. **Whole body:** Expand your awareness to take in your entire body at once. Feel the wholeness of your body breathing and resting. Stay here for a few breaths.

14. When you are ready, gently wiggle your fingers and toes. Open your eyes slowly.

## Exercise 5: Mindful Walking

**Best for:** Restlessness, getting out of your head, combining movement with mindfulness
**Duration:** 5-15 minutes

Mindful walking is an excellent practice for students who find it difficult to sit still for meditation. You can practice this while walking across campus, in a park, or even in a hallway.

### Instructions

1. Begin walking at a slower pace than usual. There is no destination — the walk itself is the practice.

2. **Feel your feet:** Pay close attention to each step. Notice the heel making contact with the ground, the weight shifting through your foot, and your toes pushing off.

3. **Notice your body:** Feel how your legs move, how your arms swing, how your balance shifts from side to side.

4. **Engage your senses:** As you walk, notice what you see around you — colors, shapes, light, and shadow. Listen to the sounds. Feel the air on your skin. Notice any smells.

5. **When your mind wanders,** gently return your attention to the physical sensation of walking. No judgment — just come back.

6. **Experiment with pace.** Try walking very slowly for a minute, then at your normal pace. Notice how the experience changes.

7. After your walk, pause for a moment. Take a breath and notice how you feel compared to when you began.

**Tip:** Try mindful walking during your commute to class. Even 5 minutes of mindful walking between classes can reset your mental state and improve focus.

---

## Building Your Practice

You do not need to do all five exercises every day. Start with one that resonates with you and practice it regularly. Even five minutes of daily mindfulness practice has been shown to produce measurable benefits within a few weeks. The key is consistency, not perfection.
