# Seeking Professional Help for Mental Health

## When Should You See a Professional?

Many mental health challenges can be managed with self-help strategies, social support, and healthy lifestyle habits. However, there are times when professional help is needed — and recognizing those times is important.

### Consider seeking professional help if:

- **Your symptoms persist.** Feelings of sadness, anxiety, or distress that last more than two weeks and are not improving warrant professional attention.
- **Daily life is affected.** You are struggling to attend classes, complete assignments, maintain relationships, or take care of basic needs like eating and sleeping.
- **Self-help is not enough.** You have tried coping strategies (breathing exercises, routine changes, talking to friends) but your symptoms continue or worsen.
- **You are using substances to cope.** Turning to alcohol, drugs, or other substances to manage your emotions is a sign that you need additional support.
- **Your thoughts are frightening you.** Persistent thoughts of death, self-harm, or suicide require immediate professional intervention.
- **Trauma is affecting you.** If past traumatic experiences are intruding on your present life through flashbacks, nightmares, or avoidance, trauma-informed therapy can help.
- **Relationships are consistently difficult.** If you find yourself in repeated unhealthy relationship patterns, a therapist can help you understand and change these patterns.
- **You feel stuck.** Sometimes life presents challenges that benefit from an objective, trained perspective.

### Important truth: You do not need to be in crisis to see a therapist.

Just as you visit a doctor for a check-up and not only when you are seriously ill, seeing a mental health professional for general wellbeing is valuable and wise. Many people benefit from therapy even when they are functioning well — it helps them grow, understand themselves better, and build resilience.

## Types of Mental Health Professionals

Understanding the different types of professionals can help you find the right support:

### Clinical Psychologists
- Hold a doctoral or master's degree in psychology
- Trained in psychological assessment and therapy
- Use evidence-based therapeutic approaches (CBT, DBT, psychodynamic therapy, etc.)
- Do not prescribe medication (in most countries)
- Best for: talk therapy, psychological assessment, specific mental health conditions

### Psychiatrists
- Medical doctors who specialize in mental health
- Can prescribe and manage psychiatric medications
- May also provide therapy, though many focus primarily on medication management
- Best for: conditions that may require medication (severe depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, ADHD)

### Counselors and Psychotherapists
- Trained in therapeutic techniques, often at master's level
- Provide talk therapy for a range of concerns
- May specialize in areas like grief, relationships, career, or trauma
- Best for: general mental health concerns, life transitions, relationship issues

### Social Workers (Clinical)
- Trained in mental health and social welfare
- Often work in community settings, hospitals, or universities
- Can provide therapy and connect you with community resources
- Best for: holistic support that considers social and economic factors

### School and University Counselors
- Based on campus and familiar with the specific challenges students face
- Usually provide short-term counseling and referrals
- Often available at no additional cost to enrolled students
- Best for: immediate, accessible support for academic and personal concerns

## What to Expect in Your First Session

Not knowing what to expect is one of the biggest barriers to seeking help. Here is what typically happens:

### Before the Session
- You may be asked to fill out a brief questionnaire about your symptoms and history
- Prepare to share what has been troubling you, but know that you do not have to share everything immediately
- Write down your main concerns or questions in advance if it helps

### During the First Session
- The therapist will introduce themselves and explain their approach and confidentiality policies
- **Confidentiality** is a cornerstone of therapy: what you share stays private, with very few exceptions (imminent risk of harm to yourself or others, and in some jurisdictions, abuse of a minor)
- You will be asked about your current concerns, background, and what you hope to get from therapy
- The therapist may ask about your family, relationships, academic life, sleep, appetite, and substance use
- You will discuss goals for therapy and how sessions will be structured
- The first session is also for you to evaluate the therapist — do you feel comfortable? Heard? Safe?

### After the First Session
- You may feel relieved, emotionally drained, or both — this is normal
- The therapist may suggest a frequency for sessions (weekly is common)
- Change takes time. Most evidence-based therapies show significant improvement within 8-20 sessions, but everyone's timeline is different

## Overcoming Barriers to Seeking Help

### Stigma
Mental health stigma is real, particularly in some cultural contexts. Remember:
- Seeking help is a sign of strength, not weakness
- Mental health conditions are medical conditions, not character flaws
- You do not have to tell anyone about your therapy if you do not want to
- Attitudes are changing, and by seeking help you contribute to normalizing mental health care

### Cost
- Check if your university offers free or subsidized counseling services
- MindBridge can help connect you with affordable or free resources
- Some therapists offer sliding scale fees based on ability to pay
- Online therapy options are often more affordable than in-person sessions
- Community mental health organizations may provide free services

### "My problem is not serious enough"
- There is no minimum level of suffering required to seek help
- Early intervention prevents problems from escalating
- Therapists work with people across the full spectrum of mental health concerns

### "I should be able to handle this on my own"
- Seeking help is a skill, not a shortcoming
- Even professional athletes have coaches. Using expert guidance is smart, not weak
- Some challenges genuinely require professional tools and perspectives

### Cultural and Language Barriers
- It is okay to ask for a therapist who understands your cultural background
- If you are more comfortable speaking in French, English, or another language, seek a therapist who can accommodate that
- You have the right to find a therapist who is a good fit for you

## Tips for Getting the Most from Therapy

- **Be honest.** Therapy works best when you share openly, even when it is uncomfortable.
- **Be patient.** Meaningful change takes time. Do not expect instant results.
- **Do the work between sessions.** Therapy often involves practicing skills or completing exercises outside of sessions.
- **Speak up if something is not working.** A good therapist wants your feedback and will adjust their approach.
- **Give it a fair chance.** Try at least 3-4 sessions before deciding if a therapist is right for you.
- **If the fit is not right, try someone else.** The therapeutic relationship matters enormously. It is okay to switch therapists.

## Remember

Taking the step to seek professional help is one of the most empowering things you can do for yourself. It means you value your own wellbeing enough to invest in it. Whatever you are going through, you deserve support — and support is available.
