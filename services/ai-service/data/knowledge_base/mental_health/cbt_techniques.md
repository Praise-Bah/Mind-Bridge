# Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) Techniques

## What Is CBT?

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is the most widely researched and evidence-based form of psychotherapy. Developed by Dr. Aaron Beck in the 1960s, CBT is based on the principle that our thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are interconnected — and that by changing unhelpful thinking patterns, we can change how we feel and act.

CBT is not about "thinking positively" or pretending everything is fine. It is about developing a more accurate, balanced, and helpful way of interpreting the events in your life.

## The CBT Model

The core idea of CBT can be summarized as:

**Situation → Thought → Emotion → Behavior**

It is not events themselves that cause our emotional distress, but rather our interpretation of those events. Two students can receive the same grade on an exam and have completely different emotional responses based on their thoughts about it.

**Example:**
- **Situation:** You receive a 60% on a midterm exam.
- **Thought A:** "I failed. I am stupid and I will never succeed." → **Emotion:** Shame, hopelessness → **Behavior:** Avoid studying, skip next class
- **Thought B:** "That was lower than I hoped. I need to change my study approach." → **Emotion:** Disappointment, determination → **Behavior:** Seek help, adjust study plan

The situation is the same. The thought makes all the difference.

## Cognitive Distortions

Cognitive distortions are habitual, biased ways of thinking that contribute to emotional distress. Everyone experiences them, but awareness is the first step to change.

### Common Cognitive Distortions

**1. All-or-Nothing Thinking**
Seeing things in black and white with no middle ground. "If I do not get an A, I have completely failed."

**2. Catastrophizing**
Jumping to the worst possible outcome. "I stumbled in my presentation — now everyone thinks I am incompetent and I will never get a job."

**3. Mind Reading**
Assuming you know what others are thinking, usually something negative. "My classmates think I am weird."

**4. Fortune Telling**
Predicting the future negatively without evidence. "I just know I am going to fail this course."

**5. Overgeneralization**
Taking a single event and applying it broadly. "I did badly on one quiz, so I am bad at everything."

**6. Mental Filtering**
Focusing exclusively on the negative while ignoring positive information. Receiving feedback that is 90% positive but fixating on the one criticism.

**7. Discounting the Positive**
Dismissing positive experiences as flukes or not counting. "I only did well because the exam was easy."

**8. Should Statements**
Rigid rules about how things should be. "I should be able to handle everything without struggling." These create guilt and frustration.

**9. Emotional Reasoning**
Assuming that because you feel something, it must be true. "I feel like a failure, therefore I am a failure."

**10. Personalization**
Taking responsibility for things that are not your fault. "My group project went badly — it must be because of me."

**11. Labeling**
Attaching a fixed label to yourself or others based on a single behavior. "I made a mistake, therefore I am an idiot."

## Thought Records

A thought record is a structured tool for examining and challenging unhelpful thoughts. It is one of the most powerful CBT techniques you can use on your own.

### How to Complete a Thought Record

Use a notebook or digital document with these columns:

| Column | What to Write |
|--------|---------------|
| **Situation** | What happened? Where were you? Who was there? |
| **Automatic Thought** | What went through your mind? What is the thought that is most upsetting? |
| **Emotion** | What emotion(s) did you feel? Rate intensity 0-100%. |
| **Evidence For** | What facts support this thought? |
| **Evidence Against** | What facts contradict this thought? |
| **Balanced Thought** | What is a more realistic, balanced way of seeing this? |
| **Outcome** | How do you feel now? Rate the emotion again 0-100%. |

### Example Thought Record

| Column | Entry |
|--------|-------|
| **Situation** | Lecturer returned my assignment with a C grade. |
| **Automatic Thought** | "I am not smart enough for this program. I should drop out." |
| **Emotion** | Shame (80%), Hopelessness (70%) |
| **Evidence For** | I got a C on this assignment. Some topics are hard for me. |
| **Evidence Against** | I got B+ on two other assignments. I was sick the week I wrote this. Other students also found it difficult. I was admitted to this program based on my qualifications. |
| **Balanced Thought** | "One C does not define my ability. I was unwell when I wrote this, and I can seek feedback to improve next time." |
| **Outcome** | Shame (30%), Hopelessness (20%), Motivation (50%) |

## Behavioral Experiments

Behavioral experiments test whether your negative predictions are accurate. They are like scientific experiments for your thoughts.

### Steps for a Behavioral Experiment

1. **Identify the prediction.** "If I ask a question in class, everyone will laugh at me."
2. **Rate your belief in the prediction** (0-100%). "I believe this 75%."
3. **Design the experiment.** "I will ask one question in tomorrow's lecture."
4. **Carry out the experiment.** Actually do it.
5. **Record what happened.** "I asked a question. The lecturer answered it seriously. Two classmates nodded. Nobody laughed."
6. **Re-rate your belief.** "I now believe 'everyone will laugh' at 15%."
7. **What did you learn?** "My fear was exaggerated. Most people are focused on learning, not judging me."

### Tips for Behavioral Experiments
- Start with lower-risk experiments and build up
- Be specific about what you are testing
- Record results honestly — both what confirms and contradicts your prediction
- Repeat experiments to build confidence

## Activity Scheduling

Activity scheduling involves planning specific activities to counteract patterns of avoidance, withdrawal, or inactivity that maintain depression and anxiety.

- Schedule a mix of **pleasure activities** (things you enjoy) and **mastery activities** (things that give you a sense of achievement)
- Rate your mood before and after each activity to identify what helps most
- Start with small, manageable activities and build from there

## Applying CBT in Daily Life

You do not need to be in formal therapy to use CBT techniques:

- **Catch your thoughts.** When you notice a strong negative emotion, pause and ask: "What just went through my mind?"
- **Write it down.** Getting thoughts on paper makes them easier to examine objectively.
- **Look for distortions.** Review the list of cognitive distortions. Can you identify any?
- **Challenge and reframe.** Generate a more balanced perspective.
- **Test your predictions.** Use behavioral experiments to check whether your fears are realistic.
- **Practice regularly.** Like any skill, CBT techniques improve with practice.
